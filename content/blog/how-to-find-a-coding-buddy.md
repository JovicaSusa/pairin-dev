Learning to code on your own is hard to keep up. When researchers at Harvard and MIT looked at millions of registrations for their free online courses, [only about 3 to 6% of participants finished](https://webs.um.es/jruiperez/papers/journals/2019_Science_MOOCPivot_postprint.pdf). Even among people who said up front that they intended to complete a course, fewer than one in five did.

A coding buddy is one of the simplest fixes: someone at a similar stage who expects you to show up, and who you can think out loud with when you're stuck. The good news is that finding one isn't the hard part. Keeping one is. This guide covers both.

## Where to look

- **People you already know.** Classmates, a bootcamp cohort, a friend or coworker who's also learning. If you have them, start here.
- **Course communities.** The [freeCodeCamp forum](https://forum.freecodecamp.org/) has regular "looking for a study partner" threads, and The Odin Project's community lives on its Discord server. Conversations that start in a forum often move to Discord once two people click.
- **Reddit.** r/ProgrammingBuddies exists for exactly this. Say what you're learning, your level and when you're free, so the right people can recognize themselves in your post.
- **Local meetups and coding dojos.** Slower to find, but meeting someone in person makes the first conversation easier.
- **Pairin.** We built [Pairin](/) for this step. More on that at the end.

If you're new to the idea of coding with someone, our [guide to pair programming](/blog/what-is-pair-programming) covers the basics.

## Why finding someone is the easy part

Post in any of those places and you'll probably get replies. People like the idea of a coding buddy. What's much rarer is a reply turning into two people who meet every week.

We see the same thing on Pairin. Of all the applications people have sent to each other's searches so far, nearly 70% never got an answer at all. Nobody said no. The conversation just never started. So the advice below is mostly about what happens after someone says "I'm in".

## What makes a good match

When we asked people on r/learnprogramming what made learning with a buddy work, the answers were practical. One reply put it in a sentence: it "works best when both people have similar goals and consistent schedules." Another opened with a warning: "If you all are about the same level, it could work, but if not, then... good luck."

That gives you three things to look for:

- **A similar level and pace.** Not identical, but close enough that neither person is always waiting or always catching up.
- **Schedules that overlap.** Time zones and working hours decide more partnerships than enthusiasm does.
- **A shared goal.** "Learning Python" is vague. "Building a small budgeting app in Python by February" gives you both something to aim at and something to talk about.

## Talk before you commit

The longest reply in that thread was a story from a bootcamp, where partners were assigned rather than chosen. One teammate went silent until two days before a deadline. Another took on a feature they couldn't build and didn't say so. Their conclusion:

> Yes, we had slack and Zoom and all that. That's not the problem.

Their advice was to have a coffee or a call first, "just to size each other up before any projects start." You don't need anything formal. One relaxed conversation covering four things will tell you most of what you need:

- **Your honest level.** What you've built, what you're stuck on, what you've only watched videos about.
- **Time.** How many hours a week each of you can really give, and which ones.
- **What you'd work on.** A project, a course, interview prep. Agree on something concrete for the first few weeks.
- **Where you'll talk.** Discord, WhatsApp, a shared repo. Pick one place so nothing gets lost.

If the answers don't line up, that's useful too. It's much easier to say "not quite a fit" after one call than after a month.

## How to keep it going

Most partnerships don't end with a decision. They fade: a missed week, then another. A few habits make that much less likely.

**Pick a fixed time.** "Let's code sometime this week" rarely survives a busy week. "Tuesdays at 7, on Discord" usually does. This isn't just intuition: a [review of 94 studies](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes) found that making a specific when-and-where plan noticeably increases how often people follow through on what they intended.

**Set small weekly targets.** Each week, say what you'll each get done, and check in on it next time. The same Reddit reply that stressed goals and schedules added that "small weekly targets and checking each other's progress" keeps the motivation alive.

**Work on the code together, not just side by side.** Sharing a screen and taking turns at the keyboard turns a weekly catch-up into real practice. One tip from the thread for whoever is watching: "Have the navigator ask the driver to explain the next step before offering a fix." It keeps both of you thinking.

**Say early if the fit changes.** People learn at different speeds, and a match that worked in week one may not in week eight. If one of you is pulling ahead or has less time, talk about it. Adjusting the plan, or ending things kindly, beats letting it go quiet.

## The short version

Finding a coding buddy takes a post. Keeping one takes a quick conversation before you start, a fixed time each week and a small plan you both check. None of that needs special tools, just two people who agree on it.
